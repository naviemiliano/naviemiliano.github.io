Project 1: Images of the Russian Empire -- Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection

Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii (1863-1944) was a Russian phottographer who traveled across the vast Russian empire to take color photographs of everything he saw.To do this he recorded three exposures of every scene onto a glass plate using a red, a green, and a blue filter. Although there was no way to print the images at the time, he showcasing them using special projectors in "multimedia" classrooms all across Russia, where the children would be able to learn about their vast country. His plans never materialized, as he left Russia in 1918, right after the revolution, and never returned again. Prokudin-Gorskii's RGB glass plate negatives, which captured the last years of the Russian Empire, were purchased in 1948—and recently digitized—by the Library of Congress.

The goal of this project is to utilize image processing techniques to reconstruct Prokudin-Gorskii’s glass-plate images and produce their colorized counterparts with as few visual artifacts as possible. To do this, I intook the digitized images and separated them into their respective RGB color channels, overlaid and aligned them to prodcude a single colorized image.

Part 1 – Naive Implementation

I started with a naive approach on the smaller images. For each image, I had the algorithm read the image and split it into it's respective RGB channels by dividing the entire image by 1/3. From there, I used an exhaustive alignment algorithm that searched over a specified window of displacements—in this case [-15, 15]—and used a scoring metric to calculate the image alignment, and return the most aligned displacement. After aligning the images, I cropped the borders to remove the chromic abberation seen along the edges of the photographs.

Scoring Metrics

For the scoring metric, I experimented with L2 Norm and Normalized Cross Correlation (NCC) to determine channel alignment.

L2 Norm, also called the Euclidean Norm, compares the pixel intensity at specified positions within the two images. It then subtracts the pixel values and squares the difference, and sums over all pixels. Although it is simple, this metric is affected by differences in light intensity (if one image is brighter than the other) and exposure. In contrast, Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC) first normalizes the two images, to account for differing brightness or contrast across both images. Because of this, NCC is comparing the two images based on intensity patterns, rather than the individual pixel values.

I initially priorized utilizing L2 Norm, as the equation was simple, however, quickly realized that although the algorithm ran quicker, the output results were less aligned in comparison to NCC.

Challenges

As you can see in the images above, although this implementation worked well for the the cathedral and tobolsk images, the monastery was still showed severe signs of misalignment. In response, I changed the order of steps. Instead of cropping the final colorized image, I instead cropped the individual RGB channels before overlaying the images. My reasoning for this was since the edges of each image contained noise, borders, or partial artifacts the inclusion of these was likely affecting the alignment metrics (L2 and NCC). Part 2 – Pyramid Implementation

Although the naive approach worked well on smaller images, it is not effective for larger images. To handle larger, higher-resolution images efficiently, I implemented a 4-level-pyramid alignment algorithm. Instead of exhaustively searching over a large displacement window on the larger full-resolution images, I built an image pyramid by repeatedly downsampling the source and reference channels.

I first aligned the smallest, coarsest level of the pyramid using exhaustive search, and then progressively worked my way up and refined the displacement estimates at higher resolutions. By creating an image puyramid, I was able to make the alignment process run significantly fater, while still returning aligned images.

Challenges

The above methodology did work for most images, however it notably did not work for the melons or self portrait image files.

Gradient-Based Alignment
In addition, I integrated a gradient-based preprocessing step. For each RGB channel, I used np.gradient to get its gradient magnitude, then used the gradient to calculate alignment. By doing this the algorithm focus on consistent features or patterns across channels, making it less sensitive to differences in exposure or lighting.

Improved Images
